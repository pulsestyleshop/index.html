Understanding Mortgage Amortization Schedules

Published on: October 25, 2026 | Written by: MortgageCalc Team

When you take out a mortgage, you commit to making a fixed monthly payment for a set number of years (usually 15 or 30). But have you ever wondered how the bank decides how much of that payment goes toward the actual house versus how much goes into their pockets as interest?

This process is called amortization. Understanding how it works is crucial, especially if you plan to pay off your loan early or want to know how much equity you have in your home.

What is an Amortization Schedule?

An amortization schedule is a table that shows exactly how each monthly payment is split between the principal (the money you borrowed) and the interest (the lender's fee). It also shows the remaining balance of your loan after every single payment.

How the Split Changes Over Time

With a standard fixed-rate mortgage, your monthly payment amount never changes. However, the breakdown of that payment changes dramatically over time.

The Early Years: Interest-Heavy

In the beginning of your loan term, the vast majority of your monthly payment goes toward interest. Why? Because interest is calculated based on the remaining balance of your loan. Since your balance is highest at the start, the interest charge is also at its highest.

Only a small fraction of your early payments actually reduce the amount you owe.

The Later Years: Principal-Heavy

As the years go by and your loan balance slowly decreases, the interest charge gets smaller. This allows a larger portion of your monthly payment to go toward the principal. By the end of your loan term, almost your entire payment is going toward paying off the remaining principal.

Did you know? On a 30-year mortgage, you often won't reach the 50/50 mark (where 50% of your payment goes to principal) until roughly year 15 or later!

Why Amortization Matters to You

Understanding this concept is important for two main reasons:

  1. Building Equity: Because so much of your early payment goes to interest, you don't build equity very fast in the first few years. If home prices drop and you need to sell in year 3, you might not have enough equity to cover the sale.
  2. The Power of Extra Payments: If you make an extra payment, you must ensure it goes directly to the principal. Because you are skipping ahead in the schedule, you save all the future interest that money would have accrued. This can shave years off your loan.

Conclusion

Amortization is just a fancy word for a repayment plan. By looking at the amortization schedule on our calculator, you can see exactly how your balance will drop year by year. This knowledge empowers you to make smart decisions, like whether to refinance or make extra payments to save on interest.

Educational Purposes Only: This article is for informational purposes only. Actual mortgage amortization schedules vary based on your specific loan terms, interest rate, and lender. Consult your lender for your exact loan schedule.
